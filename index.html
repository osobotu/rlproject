ActionComplete: Sample-Efficient Post-Training of VLAs via Residual RL

Abhikhya Tripathy1, Saksham Sharma1, Stephen Oduh1,
1Carnegie Mellon University
Paper Code

Abstract

Learning robotic manipulation policies that are both effective and sample-efficient remains a major challenge, particularly for tasks requiring precise physical interactions of the robot with the environment. Although end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) has shown promise and been widely used for such tasks, it often suffers from slow convergence, sensitivity to reward design, and suboptimal exploration in complex environments. In this work, we investigate a framework that leverages pretrained base policies with rich prior knowledge, often learned via behavior cloning (BC), and adapts them to challenging manipulation tasks through the addition of a residual policy learned via online reinforcement learning. Instead of training from scratch, the residual policy only learns small actions to correct the base policy, resulting in more effective and sample-efficient learning. We specifically use Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which are pretrained on large amounts of data, as our base policy. We further highlight limitations of naive exploration and propose more informed exploration strategies beyond low-level action imitation.

Overall Proposed Architecture

We use a frozen pretrained VLA model as a base policy that predicts coarse actions from RGB observations and language instructions. On top of it, we learn a residual RL policy online that predicts small corrective actions, which are added to the base actions. The residual policy's observation is augmented with the base policy's predicted action, allowing it to condition its corrections on what the VLA intends to do. This design preserves the VLA's broad task priors while enabling precise, environment-specific fine-tuning through RL.

Baseline Comparison: PPO vs SAC vs TD-MPC2

To establish performance baselines prior to integrating the VLA, we trained three standalone RL agents directly on PegInsertionSide-v1: PPO (on-policy policy-gradient), SAC (off-policy Q-function-based), and TD-MPC2 (model-based RL with an implicit world model and model-predictive planning). All agents use only RGB observations and the pd_joint_delta_pos control mode. SAC converges faster than PPO among model-free methods, reaching a stable return of ~25 vs PPO's ~6 at the same step count. TD-MPC2 achieves the highest early returns by planning ahead with its learned world model, though model errors introduce visible variance. All three agents learn to pick up the peg and orient it toward the hole but fail at the millimeter-precision insertion step, resulting in 0% success rate — motivating our residual RL approach with a pretrained VLA base policy.

Proposed Modifications

Modification 1: Learning Exploration Strategies from Demonstrations

A key limitation of naive RL exploration is that random actions almost never reach the critical contact states needed for peg insertion. This modification teaches the residual RL policy high-level strategies for efficient exploration from a small number of demonstrations, rather than just imitating low-level actions. We decompose peg insertion into five discrete phases — free-move, make-contact, sweep-surface, engage-hole, and insert — motivated by how a human searches for a hidden hole using surface contact. A hierarchical Q-learner builds a skill library from demonstration segments and learns inter-skill transition probabilities, forming a mechanism graph that encodes the high-level strategy. The RL exploration distribution is warm-started from this demo-conditioned distribution, biasing early exploration toward contact-search motions rather than random flailing. Our implementation takes inspiration from Learning Reusable Manipulation Strategies (J. Mao et al., 2023).

Forced Stage Progression

Here, we force the execution to move on to the next learned stage regardless of low-level success after a few timesteps, in order to visualize the algorithm's ability to learn high-level mechanisms. Even though the low-level tasks of grasping and moving the peg to the surface do not succeed, the robot gripper follows through with the high-level stages learned as part of the strategy — gripping the peg, moving it to the surface, making contact, and performing a sweep to detect the hole position. This reveals that the major bottleneck for success on fine-grained manipulation tasks is the effectiveness of the low-level controllers, validating the idea of decoupling high-level strategies from low-level actions for better generalization. Well-trained low-level controllers for basic manipulation tasks can be composed for diverse tasks in high-level sequences learned through demonstrations.

Stuck at Grasping Stage

The modification 1 algorithm tries to follow through with the high-level stages it learned as part of the learned mechanism/strategy. It learns that the first stage is grasping the peg and attempts to perform the low-level actions associated with it, but fails to do so. Hence the execution gets stuck on this stage and is unable to progress to the next learned high-level stage.

Modification 2: VLM as a Learned Reward Signal

Hand-crafted reward functions require expert knowledge, are task-specific, and risk reward hacking. This modification replaces the manually engineered dense reward with a learned visual reward model, Robometer, trained on over 1 million robot trajectory comparisons using a vision-language backbone. Robometer provides a progress score \(\hat{r}_{\text{prog}}(s_t, g) \in [0,1]\) for any visual observation and language goal, requiring no task-specific engineering. We use this as a drop-in reward for the residual RL policy. On the PickCube-v1 task, where progress is visually clear, Robometer-guided residual RL steadily improves above the VLA baseline. On PegInsertionSide-v1, it provides better early guidance than the hand-crafted reward but cannot reliably distinguish near-contact failures from successful insertion states — confirming that Robometer shines when task progress is visually observable and smoothly correlated with success.
